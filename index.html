Ask the I Ching

The I Ching (Book of Changes) is a Chinese divination manual roughly three thousand years old. You focus on a question, toss coins to build a six-line figure called a hexagram, and the I Ching offers a reading, along with any aspects that are in flux.

The aim of the I Ching is to empower your intuition and get a fresh angle on something you’ve been pondering. Instead of thinking of it as fortune-telling, use it to unblock thinking, much as you might use Brian Eno’s Oblique Strategies.

The I Ching responds best to a specific situation: pose a yes-or-no question or decision that’s been weighing on you, rather than asking how your year will go or whether you’ll be rich.

Don’t be a dope and use this page for a mental health crisis, or for decisions affecting your safety or the safety of others.

Nothing on this page is saved or sent anywhere. The throws happen in your browser, and the question you enter isn’t collected or stored.

How the coins work, if you want to know first

You need three coins. Throw all three at once and note what comes up, which gives you one line of the figure, building from the bottom up. Do that six times.

The mix of heads and tails decides the line. Three of a kind produces a changing line, either Changing Unbroken Yang or Changing Broken Yin, while a mixed throw produces a static one, Static Unbroken Yang or Static Broken Yin. The static lines simply sit in the figure. The changing lines carry extra text of their own, because they mark the places where the situation is already turning into something else.

The first throw is the bottom line of the figure and the sixth throw is the top, so a hexagram is built upward the way a building goes up.

How to use this hexagram

The main hexagram is read as the situation you are in. The bottom and top halves, called trigrams, as the inside and the outside of your situation, respectively.

The readings on this page are written in modern English and are not a translation. Each hexagram carries the traditional Judgment and Image in a panel of their own so you can see what the plain wording is based on.

For the classical text, the standard public-domain English version is James Legge's translation of 1882, available alongside the Chinese original at ctext.org and at sacred-texts.com. Richard Wilhelm's German version of 1923, translated into English by Cary Baynes in 1950, is the one most readers in English have met, and it remains in copyright.
